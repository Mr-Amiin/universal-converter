Frequently asked questions

How many metric teaspoons are in one milligram?

One Milligram equals 0.0002 metric teaspoons (assuming water-like density).

Why is the milligram used in this kind of recipe?

Nutrition labels and food-science recipes need the milligram because ingredients like sodium and vitamins are active in quantities too small to sensibly express in grams.

Is this conversion exact?

Not exactly for every ingredient. This conversion assumes water-like density (1 gram per milliliter), which is accurate for water, milk, and similar liquids, but ingredients like flour, sugar, or oil will weigh a different amount for the same volume because they pack or float differently.

Can I use this conversion for any ingredient?

Use it with care: because it relies on a water-density assumption, it's most accurate for liquids and less accurate for dry or airy ingredients, which vary by how they're packed.

When would a recipe use milligrams instead of metric teaspoons?

Milligram is typically used in measuring nutritional content (sodium, vitamins), food additives, and spice-level seasoning in scientific recipe formulation, while metric teaspoon shows up in recipes published outside the US, and medicine dosing spoons worldwide.
