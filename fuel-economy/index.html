About Fuel Economy Conversion

Fuel economy measures how efficiently a vehicle uses fuel, but different countries express that efficiency in fundamentally different, and mathematically inverse, ways. The United States and UK use miles per gallon (mpg), a 'bigger number is better' distance-per-fuel measure, while most of the rest of the world uses liters per 100 kilometers (L/100km), a 'smaller number is better' fuel-per-distance measure. Because these two approaches are inverses of each other rather than simple linear conversions, converting between mpg and L/100km requires a division step, not just a multiplication factor, which is a common source of error.

This inversion also means fuel economy conversions behave non-linearly: a fixed improvement in mpg represents a shrinking real-world fuel saving at higher mpg values, while the same L/100km improvement always represents the same real-world saving regardless of the starting point, a subtlety that makes L/100km arguably more intuitive for comparing efficiency gains. This converter handles both directions accurately, along with the distinction between US and UK gallons (which differ in size), useful for comparing a vehicle's rated efficiency across international markets or specifications.

Fuel economy conversion matters increasingly as vehicles, and the specifications describing them, cross international markets: comparing a US-market car's mpg rating against a European model's L/100km figure, or making sense of an imported vehicle's efficiency claims, requires understanding both the inverse mathematical relationship between the two systems and the size difference between US and UK gallons. Because fuel economy directly affects both cost and environmental impact, accurate conversion helps consumers make genuinely comparable efficiency judgments rather than being misled by unit-system differences alone.

Because mpg and L/100km are inverse measures rather than directly proportional ones, it's worth double-checking a conversion using the actual inverse formula rather than estimating linearly, since a rough linear guess (like assuming '30 mpg is about half of 60 mpg's L/100km value') will produce a noticeably incorrect result.

Common Fuel Economy Measurements

Fuel economy is expressed either as distance per unit of fuel (like miles per gallon) or as fuel per unit of distance (like liters per 100 km), which are inverse relationships to each other.

Conversion Formulas

  • L/100km = 235.215 ÷ mpg (US)
  • mpg (US) = 235.215 ÷ L/100km
  • L/100km = 282.481 ÷ mpg (UK)
  • km/L = 100 ÷ L/100km
  • mpg (US) to mpg (UK): multiply by 0.832674

Practical Examples

  • A car rated at 30 mpg (US) uses about 7.84 L/100km, since 235.215 ÷ 30 ≈ 7.84.
  • A car using 6 L/100km gets about 39.2 mpg (US), since 235.215 ÷ 6 ≈ 39.2.
  • A car using 5 L/100km travels 20 km per liter, since 100 ÷ 5 = 20.
  • 40 mpg (US) equals about 33.3 mpg (UK), since 40 × 0.832674 ≈ 33.3.

Frequently asked questions

Why can't I just multiply to convert mpg to L/100km?

Because mpg measures distance per unit of fuel while L/100km measures fuel per unit of distance, they're inverse quantities, not directly proportional ones. Converting between them requires dividing a constant by the value (L/100km = 235.215 ÷ mpg for US gallons), not a simple multiplication.

Why is a US mpg different from a UK mpg for the same vehicle?

A US gallon (3.78541 L) is smaller than a UK imperial gallon (4.54609 L), so the same vehicle traveling the same distance on the same amount of fuel will show a higher 'miles per gallon' figure using the larger UK gallon than using the smaller US gallon.

Is a lower L/100km always better, like a higher mpg?

Yes, for L/100km, a lower number means better fuel efficiency (less fuel used per distance), which is the opposite direction from mpg, where a higher number means better efficiency (more distance per unit of fuel).

Why do fuel economy improvements matter more at low mpg than high mpg?

Because mpg is a distance-per-fuel measure, going from 10 mpg to 20 mpg cuts fuel use in half, while going from 40 mpg to 50 mpg only reduces fuel use by 20%, even though both represent a 10 mpg improvement. This is why L/100km, where equal improvements always represent equal fuel savings, is considered more intuitive for comparing efficiency gains.

What is km/L and how does it relate to mpg?

Kilometers per liter (km/L) is a distance-per-fuel measure like mpg, just using metric units instead of miles and gallons. It's used in some countries, particularly in Asia, as an alternative to L/100km.

When Should I Use Each Fuel Economy Unit?

  • Miles per gallon (MPG) — Used in the US to advertise and compare vehicle fuel efficiency.
  • Liters per 100 km — Used across Europe and much of the world, where a lower number means better efficiency.
  • Kilometers per liter — Used in parts of Asia and Latin America as a distance-per-fuel measure.
  • MPGe — Used for electric and hybrid vehicles to express efficiency in gasoline-equivalent terms.

Common Mistakes

  • ❌ Assuming L/100km and MPG scale the same direction, when a lower L/100km means better efficiency but a lower MPG means worse efficiency.
  • ❌ Confusing US gallons with UK imperial gallons, which differ by about 20%.
  • ❌ Comparing MPG figures across countries without adjusting for different testing standards.
  • ❌ Ignoring that fuel economy conversion is nonlinear, so averaging MPG values directly produces a misleading result.

History

Miles per gallon became the standard US fuel efficiency measure in the mid-20th century as car ownership expanded and gasoline pricing made efficiency a consumer concern, eventually becoming a mandated label after the 1970s oil crises pushed fuel economy into federal regulation. Liters per 100 kilometers took hold in Europe for a subtler reason: because it directly answers the practical question of how much fuel a trip will cost, and because it scales intuitively when comparing vehicles, unlike a distance-per-volume figure that requires more mental division. As electric vehicles emerged in the 2000s and 2010s, regulators created MPGe to let consumers compare electric range and efficiency against familiar gasoline benchmarks, extending a decades-old measurement tradition into an entirely new powertrain category.
