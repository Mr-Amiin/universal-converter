Unit definitions

Hectoliter per Millisecond (hL/ms)
The hectoliter is equal to 10^2 liters (a hundred liters), formed by attaching the SI prefix 'hecto-' to the liter. This flow unit measures that volume moved every millisecond (the millisecond is equal to 10^-3 seconds (a thousandth of a second)).

Cup per Fortnight (cup/fn)
The cup us is 8 US fluid ounces (about 236.6 mL), the standard US recipe-measuring unit. This flow unit measures that volume moved every fortnight (the fortnight is 14 days (two weeks, 1,209,600 seconds)).

Unit comparison

Hectoliter per Millisecond (Metric (SI-prefixed liter)) vs Cup per Fortnight (Cooking (US))

One Hectoliter per Millisecond unit represents 5.113 × 10¹¹ times more volume-per-time than one Cup per Fortnight unit.

Hectoliter per Millisecond is commonly used in beverages, fuel, laboratory glassware, and municipal water metering, while Cup per Fortnight is commonly used in US recipe scaling and household liquid dispensing.

Because the two units differ by many orders of magnitude, use scientific notation rather than rounding to a fixed number of decimal places to avoid losing precision.

About this conversion

A flow of one hectoliter every millisecond is the same physical flow as 5.11268 × 10¹¹ cup(s) every fortnight, so this page is useful for pipeline sizing, pump and pipe selection, HVAC airflow and liquid handling, irrigation and water-supply planning, and lab or industrial process calculations that mix metric, US customary, or imperial flow units.

This conversion shows up most often in beverages, fuel, laboratory glassware, and municipal water metering: a flow reported as 10 hL/ms works out to 5.113 × 10¹² cup/fn, which is useful when a spec sheet, recipe, or regulation states a rate in one unit system and you need it in the other.

Unit history

Hectoliter: The 'hecto-' prefix (h, 10^2) dates to the original 1795 French decimal-metric system, decades before the CGPM was even established (1889).

Millisecond: The 'milli-' prefix (m, 10^-3) dates to the original 1795 French decimal-metric system, decades before the CGPM was even established (1889).

Cup us: The US cup traces to 19th-century American cookbook standardization, most notably Fannie Farmer's insistence on precise, standardized measuring cups in her 1896 cookbook, which helped fix the cup at 1/16 of a US gallon.

Fortnight: From the Old English 'feowertyne niht' ('fourteen nights'); still in everyday use in the UK, Ireland, Australia, and New Zealand.
