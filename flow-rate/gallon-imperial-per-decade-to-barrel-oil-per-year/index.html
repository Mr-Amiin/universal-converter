Unit definitions

Imperial gallon per Decades (imp gal/decade)
The gallon imperial is 4.546 liters, defined as the volume of 10 pounds of water at 62°F. This flow unit measures that volume moved every decade (the decade is 10 years).

Oil barrel per Years (bbl/yr)
The barrel oil is a unit of volume standardized at exactly 42 US gallons (158.987 liters). This flow unit measures that volume moved every year (the year is the average Gregorian calendar year, 365.2425 days (31,556,952 seconds)).

Unit comparison

Imperial gallon per Decades (Imperial) vs Oil barrel per Years (Oil and gas)

One Oil barrel per Years unit represents 349.723158 times more volume-per-time than one Imperial gallon per Decades unit.

Imperial gallon per Decades is commonly used in UK fuel-economy figures and Commonwealth plumbing/irrigation flow, while Oil barrel per Years is commonly used in crude oil and refined-product flow measurement, pipeline throughput, and well production rates.

The two units are close enough in scale that ordinary decimal rounding (4-6 decimal places) keeps results accurate for most practical uses.

A flow of one imperial gallon every decade is the same physical flow as 0.00285940458454 oil barrel(s) every year, so this page is useful for pipeline sizing, pump and pipe selection, HVAC airflow and liquid handling, irrigation and water-supply planning, and lab or industrial process calculations that mix metric, US customary, or imperial flow units.

Unit history

Gallon imperial: Established by the British Weights and Measures Act of 1824 to unify the many competing 'gallon' definitions then in use (ale gallon, wine gallon, corn gallon).

Decade: From the Greek 'deka' (ten); used in long-range planning rather than any single legal standard.

Barrel oil: The 42-gallon barrel became the informal US standard during the 1860s Pennsylvania oil boom. Early producers shipped crude in used wine, whiskey, and fish barrels of inconsistent sizes, so in 1866 the Petroleum Producers' Association settled on 42 gallons -- partly to cover spillage and evaporation losses -- and the figure stuck even after oil moved to pipelines and tankers.

Year: The Gregorian calendar's 365.2425-day average year (Pope Gregory XIII, 1582) refined the Julian year of exactly 365.25 days to better track Earth's actual orbital period.
