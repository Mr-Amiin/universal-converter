Unit definitions

Gallon per Weeks (gal/wk)
The gallon us is 3.785 liters (231 cubic inches), the standard US liquid-volume unit. This flow unit measures that volume moved every week (the week is 7 days (604,800 seconds)).

Deciliter per Days (dL/d)
The deciliter is equal to 10^-1 liters (a tenth of a liter), formed by attaching the SI prefix 'deci-' to the liter. This flow unit measures that volume moved every day (the day is 86,400 seconds by convention (one mean solar day)).

Unit comparison

Gallon per Weeks (US customary) vs Deciliter per Days (Metric (SI-prefixed liter))

One Gallon per Weeks unit represents 5.407731 times more volume-per-time than one Deciliter per Days unit.

Gallon per Weeks is commonly used in US plumbing codes (fixture GPM ratings), irrigation, and fuel dispensing, while Deciliter per Days is commonly used in beverages, fuel, laboratory glassware, and municipal water metering.

The two units are close enough in scale that ordinary decimal rounding (4-6 decimal places) keeps results accurate for most practical uses.

About this conversion

This flow rate page converts Gallon per Weeks (gal/wk) to Deciliter per Days (dL/d) using the exact volumetric flow rate factor from the Universal Converter engine. A flow of one gallon every week is the same physical flow as 5.40773112 deciliter(s) every day, so this page is useful for pipeline sizing, pump and pipe selection, HVAC airflow and liquid handling, irrigation and water-supply planning, and lab or industrial process calculations that mix metric, US customary, or imperial flow units.

This conversion shows up most often in US plumbing codes (fixture GPM ratings), irrigation, and fuel dispensing: a flow reported as 10 gal/wk works out to 54.077311 dL/d, which is useful when a spec sheet, recipe, or regulation states a rate in one unit system and you need it in the other.

Unit history

Gallon us: Derives from the old English wine gallon (231 cubic inches), which American colonies kept even after Britain switched to the larger imperial gallon in 1824.

Week: The 7-day week is not part of the SI system but derives from Babylonian and Judeo-Christian tradition, historically tied to the sun, moon, and five visible planets known to antiquity.

Deciliter: The 'deci-' prefix (d, 10^-1) dates to the original 1795 French decimal-metric system, decades before the CGPM was even established (1889).

Day: The day is humanity's oldest natural time unit, defined by Earth's rotation; civil timekeeping fixes it at exactly 86,400 SI seconds, with leap seconds periodically reconciling it to the planet's actual (slightly irregular) rotation.
