Unit definitions

Gallon per Days (gal/d)
The gallon us is 3.785 liters (231 cubic inches), the standard US liquid-volume unit. This flow unit measures that volume moved every day (the day is 86,400 seconds by convention (one mean solar day)).

Deciliter per Seconds (dL/s)
The deciliter is equal to 10^-1 liters (a tenth of a liter), formed by attaching the SI prefix 'deci-' to the liter. This flow unit measures that volume moved every second (the second is the SI base unit of time).

Unit comparison

Gallon per Days (US customary) vs Deciliter per Seconds (Metric (SI-prefixed liter))

One Deciliter per Seconds unit represents 2282.446532 times more volume-per-time than one Gallon per Days unit.

Gallon per Days is commonly used in US plumbing codes (fixture GPM ratings), irrigation, and fuel dispensing, while Deciliter per Seconds is commonly used in beverages, fuel, laboratory glassware, and municipal water metering.

The two units are close enough in scale that ordinary decimal rounding (4-6 decimal places) keeps results accurate for most practical uses.

About this conversion

This flow rate page converts Gallon per Days (gal/d) to Deciliter per Seconds (dL/s) using the exact volumetric flow rate factor from the Universal Converter engine. A flow of one gallon every day is the same physical flow as 0.000438126363889 deciliter(s) every second, so this page is useful for pipeline sizing, pump and pipe selection, HVAC airflow and liquid handling, irrigation and water-supply planning, and lab or industrial process calculations that mix metric, US customary, or imperial flow units.

This conversion shows up most often in US plumbing codes (fixture GPM ratings), irrigation, and fuel dispensing: a flow reported as 10 gal/d works out to 0.004381 dL/s, which is useful when a spec sheet, recipe, or regulation states a rate in one unit system and you need it in the other.

Unit history

Gallon us: Derives from the old English wine gallon (231 cubic inches), which American colonies kept even after Britain switched to the larger imperial gallon in 1824.

Day: The day is humanity's oldest natural time unit, defined by Earth's rotation; civil timekeeping fixes it at exactly 86,400 SI seconds, with leap seconds periodically reconciling it to the planet's actual (slightly irregular) rotation.

Deciliter: The 'deci-' prefix (d, 10^-1) dates to the original 1795 French decimal-metric system, decades before the CGPM was even established (1889).

Second: Originally 1/86,400 of a mean solar day, the second was redefined by the 13th CGPM in 1967 in terms of the cesium-133 atomic transition frequency (9,192,631,770 cycles), removing its dependence on Earth's rotation.
