Unit definitions

Gallon per Millisecond (gal/ms)
The gallon us is 3.785 liters (231 cubic inches), the standard US liquid-volume unit. This flow unit measures that volume moved every millisecond (the millisecond is equal to 10^-3 seconds (a thousandth of a second)).

Cup per Days (cup/d)
The cup us is 8 US fluid ounces (about 236.6 mL), the standard US recipe-measuring unit. This flow unit measures that volume moved every day (the day is 86,400 seconds by convention (one mean solar day)).

Unit comparison

Gallon per Millisecond (US customary) vs Cup per Days (Cooking (US))

One Gallon per Millisecond unit represents 1.382 × 10⁹ times more volume-per-time than one Cup per Days unit.

Gallon per Millisecond is commonly used in US plumbing codes (fixture GPM ratings), irrigation, and fuel dispensing, while Cup per Days is commonly used in US recipe scaling and household liquid dispensing.

Because the two units differ by many orders of magnitude, use scientific notation rather than rounding to a fixed number of decimal places to avoid losing precision.

About this conversion

This flow rate page converts Gallon per Millisecond (gal/ms) to Cup per Days (cup/d) using the exact volumetric flow rate factor from the Universal Converter engine. A flow of one gallon every millisecond is the same physical flow as 1.3824 × 10⁹ cup(s) every day, so this page is useful for pipeline sizing, pump and pipe selection, HVAC airflow and liquid handling, irrigation and water-supply planning, and lab or industrial process calculations that mix metric, US customary, or imperial flow units.

This conversion shows up most often in US plumbing codes (fixture GPM ratings), irrigation, and fuel dispensing: a flow reported as 10 gal/ms works out to 1.382 × 10¹⁰ cup/d, which is useful when a spec sheet, recipe, or regulation states a rate in one unit system and you need it in the other.

Unit history

Gallon us: Derives from the old English wine gallon (231 cubic inches), which American colonies kept even after Britain switched to the larger imperial gallon in 1824.

Millisecond: The 'milli-' prefix (m, 10^-3) dates to the original 1795 French decimal-metric system, decades before the CGPM was even established (1889).

Cup us: The US cup traces to 19th-century American cookbook standardization, most notably Fannie Farmer's insistence on precise, standardized measuring cups in her 1896 cookbook, which helped fix the cup at 1/16 of a US gallon.

Day: The day is humanity's oldest natural time unit, defined by Earth's rotation; civil timekeeping fixes it at exactly 86,400 SI seconds, with leap seconds periodically reconciling it to the planet's actual (slightly irregular) rotation.
