Unit definitions

Kiloliter per Seconds (kL/s)
The kiloliter is equal to 10^3 liters (a thousand liters), formed by attaching the SI prefix 'kilo-' to the liter. This flow unit measures that volume moved every second (the second is the SI base unit of time).

Bushel per Decades (bu/decade)
The bushel us is a customary dry-volume unit equal to 2,150.42 cubic inches (about 35.24 liters). This flow unit measures that volume moved every decade (the decade is 10 years).

Unit comparison

Kiloliter per Seconds (Metric (SI-prefixed liter)) vs Bushel per Decades (Agriculture)

One Kiloliter per Seconds unit represents 8.955 × 10⁹ times more volume-per-time than one Bushel per Decades unit.

Kiloliter per Seconds is commonly used in municipal water treatment, reservoir capacity, and utility-scale flow reporting, while Bushel per Decades is commonly used in grain elevator throughput, harvest yield rates, and crop-drying airflow.

Because the two units differ by many orders of magnitude, use scientific notation rather than rounding to a fixed number of decimal places to avoid losing precision.

About this conversion

This flow rate page converts Kiloliter per Seconds (kL/s) to Bushel per Decades (bu/decade) using the exact volumetric flow rate factor from the Universal Converter engine. A flow of one kiloliter every second is the same physical flow as 8.9551 × 10⁹ bushel(s) every decade, so this page is useful for pipeline sizing, pump and pipe selection, HVAC airflow and liquid handling, irrigation and water-supply planning, and lab or industrial process calculations that mix metric, US customary, or imperial flow units.

This conversion shows up most often in municipal water treatment, reservoir capacity, and utility-scale flow reporting: a flow reported as 10 kL/s works out to 8.955 × 10¹⁰ bu/decade, which is useful when a spec sheet, recipe, or regulation states a rate in one unit system and you need it in the other.

Unit history

Kiloliter: The 'kilo-' prefix (k, 10^3) dates to the original 1795 French decimal-metric system, decades before the CGPM was even established (1889).

Second: Originally 1/86,400 of a mean solar day, the second was redefined by the 13th CGPM in 1967 in terms of the cesium-133 atomic transition frequency (9,192,631,770 cycles), removing its dependence on Earth's rotation.

Bushel us: The US bushel descends from the medieval English Winchester bushel, a grain-measuring standard formalized in the 15th century. The United States kept the Winchester bushel even after England adopted the larger Imperial bushel in 1824, so US and Imperial bushels differ slightly to this day.

Decade: From the Greek 'deka' (ten); used in long-range planning rather than any single legal standard.
