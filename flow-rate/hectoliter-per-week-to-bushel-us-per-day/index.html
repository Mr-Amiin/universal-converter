Unit definitions

Hectoliter per Weeks (hL/wk)
The hectoliter is equal to 10^2 liters (a hundred liters), formed by attaching the SI prefix 'hecto-' to the liter. This flow unit measures that volume moved every week (the week is 7 days (604,800 seconds)).

Bushel per Days (bu/d)
The bushel us is a customary dry-volume unit equal to 2,150.42 cubic inches (about 35.24 liters). This flow unit measures that volume moved every day (the day is 86,400 seconds by convention (one mean solar day)).

Unit comparison

Hectoliter per Weeks (Metric (SI-prefixed liter)) vs Bushel per Days (Agriculture)

One Bushel per Days unit represents 2.466735 times more volume-per-time than one Hectoliter per Weeks unit.

Hectoliter per Weeks is commonly used in beverages, fuel, laboratory glassware, and municipal water metering, while Bushel per Days is commonly used in grain elevator throughput, harvest yield rates, and crop-drying airflow.

The two units are close enough in scale that ordinary decimal rounding (4-6 decimal places) keeps results accurate for most practical uses.

A flow of one hectoliter every week is the same physical flow as 0.405394189406 bushel(s) every day, so this page is useful for pipeline sizing, pump and pipe selection, HVAC airflow and liquid handling, irrigation and water-supply planning, and lab or industrial process calculations that mix metric, US customary, or imperial flow units.

Unit history

Hectoliter: The 'hecto-' prefix (h, 10^2) dates to the original 1795 French decimal-metric system, decades before the CGPM was even established (1889).

Week: The 7-day week is not part of the SI system but derives from Babylonian and Judeo-Christian tradition, historically tied to the sun, moon, and five visible planets known to antiquity.

Bushel us: The US bushel descends from the medieval English Winchester bushel, a grain-measuring standard formalized in the 15th century. The United States kept the Winchester bushel even after England adopted the larger Imperial bushel in 1824, so US and Imperial bushels differ slightly to this day.

Day: The day is humanity's oldest natural time unit, defined by Earth's rotation; civil timekeeping fixes it at exactly 86,400 SI seconds, with leap seconds periodically reconciling it to the planet's actual (slightly irregular) rotation.
