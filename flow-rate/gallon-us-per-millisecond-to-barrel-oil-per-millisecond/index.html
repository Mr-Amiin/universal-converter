Unit definitions

Gallon per Millisecond (gal/ms)
The gallon us is 3.785 liters (231 cubic inches), the standard US liquid-volume unit. This flow unit measures that volume moved every millisecond (the millisecond is equal to 10^-3 seconds (a thousandth of a second)).

Oil barrel per Millisecond (bbl/ms)
The barrel oil is a unit of volume standardized at exactly 42 US gallons (158.987 liters). This flow unit measures that volume moved every millisecond (the millisecond is equal to 10^-3 seconds (a thousandth of a second)).

Unit comparison

Gallon per Millisecond (US customary) vs Oil barrel per Millisecond (Oil and gas)

One Oil barrel per Millisecond unit represents 42 times more volume-per-time than one Gallon per Millisecond unit.

Gallon per Millisecond is commonly used in US plumbing codes (fixture GPM ratings), irrigation, and fuel dispensing, while Oil barrel per Millisecond is commonly used in crude oil and refined-product flow measurement, pipeline throughput, and well production rates.

The two units are close enough in scale that ordinary decimal rounding (4-6 decimal places) keeps results accurate for most practical uses.

About this conversion

A flow of one gallon every millisecond is the same physical flow as 0.0238095238095 oil barrel(s) every millisecond, so this page is useful for pipeline sizing, pump and pipe selection, HVAC airflow and liquid handling, irrigation and water-supply planning, and lab or industrial process calculations that mix metric, US customary, or imperial flow units.

Unit history

Gallon us: Derives from the old English wine gallon (231 cubic inches), which American colonies kept even after Britain switched to the larger imperial gallon in 1824.

Millisecond: The 'milli-' prefix (m, 10^-3) dates to the original 1795 French decimal-metric system, decades before the CGPM was even established (1889).

Barrel oil: The 42-gallon barrel became the informal US standard during the 1860s Pennsylvania oil boom. Early producers shipped crude in used wine, whiskey, and fish barrels of inconsistent sizes, so in 1866 the Petroleum Producers' Association settled on 42 gallons -- partly to cover spillage and evaporation losses -- and the figure stuck even after oil moved to pipelines and tankers.
