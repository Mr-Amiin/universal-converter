Unit definitions

Kiloliter per Months (kL/mo)
The kiloliter is equal to 10^3 liters (a thousand liters), formed by attaching the SI prefix 'kilo-' to the liter. This flow unit measures that volume moved every month (the month is an average calendar month, taken as 1/12 of the 365.2425-day Gregorian year (about 30.44 days)).

Oil barrel per Hours (bbl/h)
The barrel oil is a unit of volume standardized at exactly 42 US gallons (158.987 liters). This flow unit measures that volume moved every hour (the hour is 60 minutes (3,600 seconds)).

Unit comparison

Kiloliter per Months (Metric (SI-prefixed liter)) vs Oil barrel per Hours (Oil and gas)

One Oil barrel per Hours unit represents 116.137834 times more volume-per-time than one Kiloliter per Months unit.

Kiloliter per Months is commonly used in municipal water treatment, reservoir capacity, and utility-scale flow reporting, while Oil barrel per Hours is commonly used in crude oil and refined-product flow measurement, pipeline throughput, and well production rates.

The two units are close enough in scale that ordinary decimal rounding (4-6 decimal places) keeps results accurate for most practical uses.

Unit history

Kiloliter: The 'kilo-' prefix (k, 10^3) dates to the original 1795 French decimal-metric system, decades before the CGPM was even established (1889).

Month: Calendar months originally tracked the roughly 29.5-day lunar (synodic) cycle; because civil months now vary from 28 to 31 days, unit converters use the year-based average for a single consistent value.

Barrel oil: The 42-gallon barrel became the informal US standard during the 1860s Pennsylvania oil boom. Early producers shipped crude in used wine, whiskey, and fish barrels of inconsistent sizes, so in 1866 the Petroleum Producers' Association settled on 42 gallons -- partly to cover spillage and evaporation losses -- and the figure stuck even after oil moved to pipelines and tankers.

Hour: Traces to the same Babylonian sexagesimal system as the minute; the modern equal-length hour became standard only after mechanical clocks replaced sundials in the 14th century.
