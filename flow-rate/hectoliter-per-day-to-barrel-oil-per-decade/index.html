Unit definitions

Hectoliter per Days (hL/d)
The hectoliter is equal to 10^2 liters (a hundred liters), formed by attaching the SI prefix 'hecto-' to the liter. This flow unit measures that volume moved every day (the day is 86,400 seconds by convention (one mean solar day)).

Oil barrel per Decades (bbl/decade)
The barrel oil is a unit of volume standardized at exactly 42 US gallons (158.987 liters). This flow unit measures that volume moved every decade (the decade is 10 years).

Unit comparison

Hectoliter per Days (Metric (SI-prefixed liter)) vs Oil barrel per Decades (Oil and gas)

One Hectoliter per Days unit represents 2297.30621 times more volume-per-time than one Oil barrel per Decades unit.

Hectoliter per Days is commonly used in beverages, fuel, laboratory glassware, and municipal water metering, while Oil barrel per Decades is commonly used in crude oil and refined-product flow measurement, pipeline throughput, and well production rates.

The two units are close enough in scale that ordinary decimal rounding (4-6 decimal places) keeps results accurate for most practical uses.

A flow of one hectoliter every day is the same physical flow as 2297.30621032 oil barrel(s) every decade, so this page is useful for pipeline sizing, pump and pipe selection, HVAC airflow and liquid handling, irrigation and water-supply planning, and lab or industrial process calculations that mix metric, US customary, or imperial flow units.

Unit history

Hectoliter: The 'hecto-' prefix (h, 10^2) dates to the original 1795 French decimal-metric system, decades before the CGPM was even established (1889).

Day: The day is humanity's oldest natural time unit, defined by Earth's rotation; civil timekeeping fixes it at exactly 86,400 SI seconds, with leap seconds periodically reconciling it to the planet's actual (slightly irregular) rotation.

Barrel oil: The 42-gallon barrel became the informal US standard during the 1860s Pennsylvania oil boom. Early producers shipped crude in used wine, whiskey, and fish barrels of inconsistent sizes, so in 1866 the Petroleum Producers' Association settled on 42 gallons -- partly to cover spillage and evaporation losses -- and the figure stuck even after oil moved to pipelines and tankers.

Decade: From the Greek 'deka' (ten); used in long-range planning rather than any single legal standard.
