Unit definitions

Kiloliter per Years (kL/yr)
The kiloliter is equal to 10^3 liters (a thousand liters), formed by attaching the SI prefix 'kilo-' to the liter. This flow unit measures that volume moved every year (the year is the average Gregorian calendar year, 365.2425 days (31,556,952 seconds)).

Gallon per Days (gal/d)
The gallon us is 3.785 liters (231 cubic inches), the standard US liquid-volume unit. This flow unit measures that volume moved every day (the day is 86,400 seconds by convention (one mean solar day)).

Unit comparison

Kiloliter per Years (Metric (SI-prefixed liter)) vs Gallon per Days (US customary)

One Gallon per Days unit represents 1.382593 times more volume-per-time than one Kiloliter per Years unit.

Kiloliter per Years is commonly used in municipal water treatment, reservoir capacity, and utility-scale flow reporting, while Gallon per Days is commonly used in US plumbing codes (fixture GPM ratings), irrigation, and fuel dispensing.

The two units are close enough in scale that ordinary decimal rounding (4-6 decimal places) keeps results accurate for most practical uses.

About this conversion

A flow of one kiloliter every year is the same physical flow as 0.723278513202 gallon(s) every day, so this page is useful for pipeline sizing, pump and pipe selection, HVAC airflow and liquid handling, irrigation and water-supply planning, and lab or industrial process calculations that mix metric, US customary, or imperial flow units.

Unit history

Kiloliter: The 'kilo-' prefix (k, 10^3) dates to the original 1795 French decimal-metric system, decades before the CGPM was even established (1889).

Year: The Gregorian calendar's 365.2425-day average year (Pope Gregory XIII, 1582) refined the Julian year of exactly 365.25 days to better track Earth's actual orbital period.

Gallon us: Derives from the old English wine gallon (231 cubic inches), which American colonies kept even after Britain switched to the larger imperial gallon in 1824.

Day: The day is humanity's oldest natural time unit, defined by Earth's rotation; civil timekeeping fixes it at exactly 86,400 SI seconds, with leap seconds periodically reconciling it to the planet's actual (slightly irregular) rotation.
