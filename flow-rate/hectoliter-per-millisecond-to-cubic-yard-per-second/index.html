Unit definitions

Hectoliter per Millisecond (hL/ms)
The hectoliter is equal to 10^2 liters (a hundred liters), formed by attaching the SI prefix 'hecto-' to the liter. This flow unit measures that volume moved every millisecond (the millisecond is equal to 10^-3 seconds (a thousandth of a second)).

Cubic yard per Seconds (yd3/s)
The cubic yard is 764.55 liters (27 cubic feet), a bulk volume unit. This flow unit measures that volume moved every second (the second is the SI base unit of time).

Unit comparison

Hectoliter per Millisecond (Metric (SI-prefixed liter)) vs Cubic yard per Seconds (Imperial/US engineering)

One Hectoliter per Millisecond unit represents 130.795062 times more volume-per-time than one Cubic yard per Seconds unit.

Hectoliter per Millisecond is commonly used in beverages, fuel, laboratory glassware, and municipal water metering, while Cubic yard per Seconds is commonly used in concrete pour rates, earthmoving, and bulk aggregate delivery.

The two units are close enough in scale that ordinary decimal rounding (4-6 decimal places) keeps results accurate for most practical uses.

About this conversion

A flow of one hectoliter every millisecond is the same physical flow as 130.795061931 cubic yard(s) every second, so this page is useful for pipeline sizing, pump and pipe selection, HVAC airflow and liquid handling, irrigation and water-supply planning, and lab or industrial process calculations that mix metric, US customary, or imperial flow units.

This conversion shows up most often in beverages, fuel, laboratory glassware, and municipal water metering: a flow reported as 10 hL/ms works out to 1307.950619 yd3/s, which is useful when a spec sheet, recipe, or regulation states a rate in one unit system and you need it in the other.

Unit history

Hectoliter: The 'hecto-' prefix (h, 10^2) dates to the original 1795 French decimal-metric system, decades before the CGPM was even established (1889).

Millisecond: The 'milli-' prefix (m, 10^-3) dates to the original 1795 French decimal-metric system, decades before the CGPM was even established (1889).

Cubic yard: The cubic yard follows from the English yard, standardized under King Edward I in the 13th century, and remains the standard billing unit for ready-mix concrete delivery in the United States.

Second: Originally 1/86,400 of a mean solar day, the second was redefined by the 13th CGPM in 1967 in terms of the cesium-133 atomic transition frequency (9,192,631,770 cycles), removing its dependence on Earth's rotation.
