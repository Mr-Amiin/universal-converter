Unit definitions

Megaliter per Days (ML/d)
The megaliter is equal to 10^6 liters (a million liters), formed by attaching the SI prefix 'mega-' to the liter. This flow unit measures that volume moved every day (the day is 86,400 seconds by convention (one mean solar day)).

Microliter per Weeks (uL/wk)
The microliter is equal to 10^-6 liters (a millionth of a liter), formed by attaching the SI prefix 'micro-' to the liter. This flow unit measures that volume moved every week (the week is 7 days (604,800 seconds)).

Unit comparison

Megaliter per Days (Metric (SI-prefixed liter)) vs Microliter per Weeks (Metric (SI-prefixed liter))

One Megaliter per Days unit represents 7 × 10¹² times more volume-per-time than one Microliter per Weeks unit.

Megaliter per Days is commonly used in municipal water treatment, reservoir capacity, and utility-scale flow reporting, while Microliter per Weeks is commonly used in laboratory dosing, IV infusion rates, and microfluidic/pharmaceutical dispensing.

Because the two units differ by many orders of magnitude, use scientific notation rather than rounding to a fixed number of decimal places to avoid losing precision.

About this conversion

This flow rate page converts Megaliter per Days (ML/d) to Microliter per Weeks (uL/wk) using the exact volumetric flow rate factor from the Universal Converter engine. A flow of one megaliter every day is the same physical flow as 7 × 10¹² microliter(s) every week, so this page is useful for pipeline sizing, pump and pipe selection, HVAC airflow and liquid handling, irrigation and water-supply planning, and lab or industrial process calculations that mix metric, US customary, or imperial flow units.

This conversion shows up most often in municipal water treatment, reservoir capacity, and utility-scale flow reporting: a flow reported as 10 ML/d works out to 7 × 10¹³ uL/wk, which is useful when a spec sheet, recipe, or regulation states a rate in one unit system and you need it in the other.

Unit history

Megaliter: The 'mega-' prefix (M, 10^6) was adopted by the 11th CGPM in 1960.

Day: The day is humanity's oldest natural time unit, defined by Earth's rotation; civil timekeeping fixes it at exactly 86,400 SI seconds, with leap seconds periodically reconciling it to the planet's actual (slightly irregular) rotation.

Microliter: The 'micro-' prefix (u, 10^-6) was adopted by the 11th CGPM in 1960.

Week: The 7-day week is not part of the SI system but derives from Babylonian and Judeo-Christian tradition, historically tied to the sun, moon, and five visible planets known to antiquity.
