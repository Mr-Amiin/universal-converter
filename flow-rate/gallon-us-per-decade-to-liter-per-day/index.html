Unit definitions

Gallon per Decades (gal/decade)
The gallon us is 3.785 liters (231 cubic inches), the standard US liquid-volume unit. This flow unit measures that volume moved every decade (the decade is 10 years).

Liter per Days (L/d)
The liter is a non-SI metric unit of volume equal to exactly one cubic decimeter (0.001 m^3). This flow unit measures that volume moved every day (the day is 86,400 seconds by convention (one mean solar day)).

Unit comparison

Gallon per Decades (US customary) vs Liter per Days (Metric)

One Liter per Days unit represents 964.868608 times more volume-per-time than one Gallon per Decades unit.

Gallon per Decades is commonly used in US plumbing codes (fixture GPM ratings), irrigation, and fuel dispensing, while Liter per Days is commonly used in beverages, fuel, laboratory glassware, and municipal water metering.

The two units are close enough in scale that ordinary decimal rounding (4-6 decimal places) keeps results accurate for most practical uses.

About this conversion

This flow rate page converts Gallon per Decades (gal/decade) to Liter per Days (L/d) using the exact volumetric flow rate factor from the Universal Converter engine. A flow of one gallon every decade is the same physical flow as 0.00103641054478 liter(s) every day, so this page is useful for pipeline sizing, pump and pipe selection, HVAC airflow and liquid handling, irrigation and water-supply planning, and lab or industrial process calculations that mix metric, US customary, or imperial flow units.

This conversion shows up most often in US plumbing codes (fixture GPM ratings), irrigation, and fuel dispensing: a flow reported as 10 gal/decade works out to 0.010364 L/d, which is useful when a spec sheet, recipe, or regulation states a rate in one unit system and you need it in the other.

Unit history

Gallon us: Derives from the old English wine gallon (231 cubic inches), which American colonies kept even after Britain switched to the larger imperial gallon in 1824.

Decade: From the Greek 'deka' (ten); used in long-range planning rather than any single legal standard.

Liter: Introduced in 1795 during French Revolutionary metrication, the liter was briefly redefined in 1901 as the volume of 1 kg of pure water at maximum density (making it 1.000028 dm^3) before the 12th CGPM restored the simpler cubic-decimeter definition in 1964.

Day: The day is humanity's oldest natural time unit, defined by Earth's rotation; civil timekeeping fixes it at exactly 86,400 SI seconds, with leap seconds periodically reconciling it to the planet's actual (slightly irregular) rotation.
