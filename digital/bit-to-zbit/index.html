Frequently asked questions

How do I convert bits to zettabits?

Multiply the bit value by 1 × 10-21. For example, 4 bit × 1 × 10-21 = 4 × 10-21 Zb.

How do I convert zettabits back to bits?

Divide the zettabit value by 1 × 10-21, which is the same as multiplying by 1 × 1021.

Why do we need a unit as large as the zettabit?

The zettabit (Zb) exists because close to the world's total annual data creation today. At that scale, smaller units like gigabytes or terabytes would require unwieldy strings of digits, so the larger prefix keeps the numbers readable.

Why measure data in bits at all?

The bit is the foundational unit every larger digital storage unit is built from. It's used directly in logic gates and processor registers and raw signal encoding, where working with a whole larger unit wouldn't make sense.

Is the bit to zettabit conversion exact?

Yes. Both units use fixed, defined prefixes (decimal and/or binary), so the conversion factor is exact rather than a rounded, measurement-based approximation.

Where is this conversion used in practice?

It comes up when comparing logic gates and processor registers, raw signal encoding, error-correction bits.

When would I use zettabits instead of bits?

Use whichever unit keeps the number easiest to read for the context: a single on/off flag, like one pixel's binary state is usually described in bits, while close to the world's total annual data creation today reads more naturally in zettabits.

About this conversion

This page converts bits (bit), the fundamental binary digit, the smallest unit of digital storage, representing a single 0 or 1, into zettabits (Zb), a decimal (SI) unit of digital storage equal to 1021 bits.

This conversion is used when comparing logic gates and processor registers and global annual data creation estimates.

The formula is Zb = bit × 1 × 10-21, based on the fixed relationship between these two digital storage units.

The term “bit” dates to 1948, coined by statistician John Tukey as a contraction of “binary digit”. The “zetta” prefix was added at the 19th CGPM to describe emerging global data volumes, standardized in 1991.

Digital storage prefixes follow the International System of Units (SI) for decimal units and the International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC) recommendations for binary units, keeping decimal and binary quantities unambiguous.
