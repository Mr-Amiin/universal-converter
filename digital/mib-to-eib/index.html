Frequently asked questions

How do I convert mebibytes to exbibytes?

Multiply the mebibyte value by 9.094947 × 10-13. For example, 4 MiB × 9.094947 × 10-13 = 3.637979 × 10-12 EiB.

How do I convert exbibytes back to mebibytes?

Divide the exbibyte value by 9.094947 × 10-13, which is the same as multiplying by 1,099,511,627,776.

Is the mebibyte to exbibyte conversion exact?

Yes. Both units use fixed, defined prefixes (decimal and/or binary), so the conversion factor is exact rather than a rounded, measurement-based approximation, though remember decimal and binary prefixes diverge further apart as the scale grows.

Where is this conversion used in practice?

It comes up when comparing photos and short audio clips, web page assets, mobile app downloads.

When would I use exbibytes instead of mebibytes?

Use whichever unit keeps the number easiest to read for the context: a compressed photo or about a minute of streamed music is usually described in mebibytes, while roughly the scale of all data crossing the internet in a single day worldwide reads more naturally in exbibytes.

About this conversion

This page converts mebibytes (MiB), a binary (IEC) unit of digital storage equal to 220 bytes, into exbibytes (EiB), a binary (IEC) unit of digital storage equal to 260 bytes.

This conversion is used when comparing photos and short audio clips and national internet traffic measurements.

The formula is EiB = MiB × 9.094947 × 10-13, based on the fixed relationship between these two digital storage units.

The “mebi” binary prefix was introduced by the International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC 60027-2) to remove the ambiguity of reusing decimal SI prefixes for binary, power-of-two computer quantities, standardized in 1998. The “exbi” binary prefix was introduced by the International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC 60027-2) to remove the ambiguity of reusing decimal SI prefixes for binary, power-of-two computer quantities, standardized in 1998.

Digital storage prefixes follow the International System of Units (SI) for decimal units and the International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC) recommendations for binary units, keeping decimal and binary quantities unambiguous.
