Frequently asked questions

How do I convert pebibytes to gigabytes?

Multiply the pebibyte value by 1,125,899.906842624. For example, 4 PiB × 1,125,899.906842624 = 4,503,599.627370496 GB.

How do I convert gigabytes back to pebibytes?

Divide the gigabyte value by 1,125,899.906842624, which is the same as multiplying by 8.881784 × 10-7.

Is the pebibyte to gigabyte conversion exact?

Yes. Both units use fixed, defined prefixes (decimal and/or binary), so the conversion factor is exact rather than a rounded, measurement-based approximation, though remember decimal and binary prefixes diverge further apart as the scale grows.

Where is this conversion used in practice?

It comes up when comparing enterprise data centers, cloud storage platforms, large scientific datasets.

When would I use gigabytes instead of pebibytes?

Use whichever unit keeps the number easiest to read for the context: a mid-size corporate data center, or a major streaming service's video catalog is usually described in pebibytes, while a two-hour HD movie download, or a phone's entire photo library reads more naturally in gigabytes.

About this conversion

This page converts pebibytes (PiB), a binary (IEC) unit of digital storage equal to 250 bytes, into gigabytes (GB), a decimal (SI) unit of digital storage equal to 109 bytes.

This conversion is used when comparing enterprise data centers and HD video files.

The formula is GB = PiB × 1,125,899.906842624, based on the fixed relationship between these two digital storage units.

The “pebi” binary prefix was introduced by the International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC 60027-2) to remove the ambiguity of reusing decimal SI prefixes for binary, power-of-two computer quantities, standardized in 1998. The “giga” prefix was formalized at the 11th General Conference on Weights and Measures (CGPM), standardized in 1960.

Digital storage prefixes follow the International System of Units (SI) for decimal units and the International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC) recommendations for binary units, keeping decimal and binary quantities unambiguous.
