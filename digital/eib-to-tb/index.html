Frequently asked questions

What is the source unit in this conversion?

The source unit is the exbibyte (EiB), a binary (IEC) unit of digital storage equal to 1,024 pebibytes, or 2^60 bytes.

What is the destination unit in this conversion?

The destination unit is the terabyte (TB), a decimal unit of digital storage equal to 1,000 gigabytes, or 10^12 bytes.

How do I convert exbibytes to terabytes?

Multiply the exbibyte value by 1152921.504606846976 to get terabytes. For example, 4 EiB × 1152921.504606846976 = 4611686.018427387904 TB.

Is the exbibyte to terabyte conversion exact?

It's exact given the fixed definitions used here: decimal storage prefixes are based on powers of 1,000, while binary storage prefixes are based on powers of 1,024. The two scales are related by an exact factor, but they should not be confused with each other in casual usage, since a decimal and a binary unit with a similar-looking name represent different amounts of data.

Where is this conversion commonly used?

It's used for describing file sizes, storage drive capacity, RAM, network bandwidth, and internet data plans or downloads.

Can I convert terabytes back to exbibytes?

Yes. Divide the terabyte value by 1152921.504606846976 to get exbibytes.

About this conversion

This page converts exbibytes (EiB), a binary (IEC) unit of digital storage equal to 1,024 pebibytes, or 2^60 bytes, into terabytes (TB), a decimal unit of digital storage equal to 1,000 gigabytes, or 10^12 bytes.

This conversion is used constantly when checking file sizes, storage drive and memory capacity, network bandwidth, and mobile or broadband data plan allowances.

The formula is TB = EiB × 1152921.504606846976, based on the fixed relationship between these two digital storage units.

It's exact given the fixed definitions used here: decimal storage prefixes are based on powers of 1,000, while binary storage prefixes are based on powers of 1,024. The two scales are related by an exact factor, but they should not be confused with each other in casual usage, since a decimal and a binary unit with a similar-looking name represent different amounts of data.

Digital storage prefixes follow the International System of Units (SI) for decimal units and the International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC) recommendations for binary units, keeping decimal and binary quantities unambiguous.
