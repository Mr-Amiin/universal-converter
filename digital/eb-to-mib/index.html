Formula

EB = MiB ÷ 953,674,316,406.25

Exact under each unit's own definition, but note EB is decimal (SI) while MiB is binary (IEC), so the two prefix families diverge at larger scales.

Frequently asked questions

How do I convert exabytes to mebibytes?

Multiply the exabyte value by 953,674,316,406.25. For example, 4 EB × 953,674,316,406.25 = 3,814,697,265,625 MiB.

How do I convert mebibytes back to exabytes?

Divide the mebibyte value by 953,674,316,406.25, which is the same as multiplying by 1.048576 × 10-12.

Is the exabyte to mebibyte conversion exact?

Yes. Both units use fixed, defined prefixes (decimal and/or binary), so the conversion factor is exact rather than a rounded, measurement-based approximation, though remember decimal and binary prefixes diverge further apart as the scale grows.

Where is this conversion used in practice?

It comes up when comparing national internet traffic measurements, large cloud provider capacity, genomic and physics datasets.

When would I use mebibytes instead of exabytes?

Use whichever unit keeps the number easiest to read for the context: roughly the scale of all data crossing the internet in a single day worldwide is usually described in exabytes, while a compressed photo or about a minute of streamed music reads more naturally in mebibytes.

About this conversion

This page converts exabytes (EB), a decimal (SI) unit of digital storage equal to 1018 bytes, into mebibytes (MiB), a binary (IEC) unit of digital storage equal to 220 bytes.

This conversion is used when comparing national internet traffic measurements and photos and short audio clips.

The formula is MiB = EB × 953,674,316,406.25, based on the fixed relationship between these two digital storage units.

The “exa” prefix was added alongside peta at the 15th CGPM, standardized in 1975. The “mebi” binary prefix was introduced by the International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC 60027-2) to remove the ambiguity of reusing decimal SI prefixes for binary, power-of-two computer quantities, standardized in 1998.

Digital storage prefixes follow the International System of Units (SI) for decimal units and the International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC) recommendations for binary units, keeping decimal and binary quantities unambiguous.
