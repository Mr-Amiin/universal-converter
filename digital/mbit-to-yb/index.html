Frequently asked questions

How do I convert megabits to yottabytes?

Multiply the megabit value by 1.25 × 10-19. For example, 4 Mb × 1.25 × 10-19 = 5 × 10-19 YB.

How do I convert yottabytes back to megabits?

Divide the yottabyte value by 1.25 × 10-19, which is the same as multiplying by 8 × 1018.

Why does it matter whether this is measured in bits or bytes?

A byte is defined as exactly 8 bits, so every bit–byte conversion involves that fixed ×8 factor. Bits are the standard for network and internet connection speeds (e.g. Mbps), while bytes are the standard for file sizes and storage capacity — which is why a fast “Mb” connection and a large “MB” file are easy to mix up.

Why do we need a unit as large as the yottabyte?

The yottabyte (YB) exists because far beyond any data volume humanity currently produces in a year. At that scale, smaller units like gigabytes or terabytes would require unwieldy strings of digits, so the larger prefix keeps the numbers readable.

Is the megabit to yottabyte conversion exact?

Yes. Both units use fixed, defined prefixes (decimal and/or binary), so the conversion factor is exact rather than a rounded, measurement-based approximation.

Where is this conversion used in practice?

It comes up when comparing photos and short audio clips, web page assets, mobile app downloads.

When would I use yottabytes instead of megabits?

Use whichever unit keeps the number easiest to read for the context: a compressed photo or about a minute of streamed music is usually described in megabits, while far beyond any data volume humanity currently produces in a year reads more naturally in yottabytes.

About this conversion

This page converts megabits (Mb), a decimal (SI) unit of digital storage equal to 106 bits, into yottabytes (YB), a decimal (SI) unit of digital storage equal to 1024 bytes.

This conversion is used when comparing photos and short audio clips and theoretical/future-scale data projections.

The formula is YB = Mb × 1.25 × 10-19, based on the fixed relationship between these two digital storage units.

The “mega” prefix was adopted by the British Association for the Advancement of Science, standardized in 1873. The “yotta” prefix was added alongside zetta at the 19th CGPM, standardized in 1991.

Digital storage prefixes follow the International System of Units (SI) for decimal units and the International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC) recommendations for binary units, keeping decimal and binary quantities unambiguous.
