How do I convert pebibytes back to zettabits?

Why does it matter whether this is measured in bits or bytes?

A byte is defined as exactly 8 bits, so every bit–byte conversion involves that fixed ×8 factor. Bits are the standard for network and internet connection speeds (e.g. Mbps), while bytes are the standard for file sizes and storage capacity — which is why a fast “Mb” connection and a large “MB” file are easy to mix up.

Why do we need a unit as large as the pebibyte?

The pebibyte (PiB) exists because a mid-size corporate data center, or a major streaming service's video catalog. At that scale, smaller units like gigabytes or terabytes would require unwieldy strings of digits, so the larger prefix keeps the numbers readable.

Is the zettabit to pebibyte conversion exact?

Yes. Both units use fixed, defined prefixes (decimal and/or binary), so the conversion factor is exact rather than a rounded, measurement-based approximation, though remember decimal and binary prefixes diverge further apart as the scale grows.

When would I use pebibytes instead of zettabits?

Use whichever unit keeps the number easiest to read for the context: close to the world's total annual data creation today is usually described in zettabits, while a mid-size corporate data center, or a major streaming service's video catalog reads more naturally in pebibytes.

About this conversion

This page converts zettabits (Zb), a decimal (SI) unit of digital storage equal to 1021 bits, into pebibytes (PiB), a binary (IEC) unit of digital storage equal to 250 bytes.

This conversion is used when comparing global annual data creation estimates and enterprise data centers.

The formula is PiB = Zb × 111,022.30246251565404236316680908203125, based on the fixed relationship between these two digital storage units.

The “zetta” prefix was added at the 19th CGPM to describe emerging global data volumes, standardized in 1991. The “pebi” binary prefix was introduced by the International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC 60027-2) to remove the ambiguity of reusing decimal SI prefixes for binary, power-of-two computer quantities, standardized in 1998.

Digital storage prefixes follow the International System of Units (SI) for decimal units and the International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC) recommendations for binary units, keeping decimal and binary quantities unambiguous.
