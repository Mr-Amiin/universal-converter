Unit definitions

Grain (gr): A grain is the smallest common avoirdupois mass unit, originally based on the mass of a single grain of barley or wheat.

Quart (US) (qt): The US liquid quart is 1/4 of a US gallon, or two US pints.

Ounce (oz): The avoirdupois ounce is 1/16 of a pound, the everyday ounce used for food and retail goods.

Cubic yard (yd³): A cubic yard is the volume of a cube with 1-yard sides, commonly used for bulk materials like concrete and soil.

Frequently asked questions

How do you convert grain per quart (US) to ounce per cubic yard?

Multiply the grain per quart (US) value by 1.846619666, since 1 gr/qt = 1.846619666 oz/yd³. This works because grain per quart (US) and ounce per cubic yard both describe mass per volume, so the conversion factor is simply the ratio of (Grain in kg / Quart (US) in liters) to (Ounce in kg / Cubic yard in liters).

How do I convert ounce per cubic yard back to grain per quart (US)?

Multiply by 0.5415300283, or equivalently divide by 1.846619666, since 1 oz/yd³ = 0.5415300283 gr/qt.

Why is grain used to measure mass here?

A grain is the smallest common avoirdupois mass unit, originally based on the mass of a single grain of barley or wheat. It is common in ammunition reloading, archery, and pharmacology, which is why densities are often reported using grain.

Why is quart (US) used as the volume unit?

The US liquid quart is 1/4 of a US gallon, or two US pints. Quart (US) shows up most often in US dairy, cooking, and automotive fluid packaging.

Why are such extremely small mass units used for density?

Grain is extremely small on its own, but density conversions often need it because ammunition reloading, archery, and pharmacology routinely deal with concentrations or masses at that scale, and using a matched unit avoids writing out long strings of zeros.

Where is the grain per quart (US) to ounce per cubic yard conversion used?

It comes up most often when moving between ammunition reloading, archery, and pharmacology on one side and US food packaging, retail, and postal weight limits on the other, or whenever a quart (US)-based recipe or spec needs to be re-expressed in cubic yard terms.
