Frequently asked questions

What is the source unit in this conversion?

The source unit is the exagray (EGy). Exagray is 1000000000000000000 gray units.

What is the destination unit in this conversion?

The destination unit is the rem (rem). Rem is 0.01 sievert.

How do I convert exagray to rem?

Multiply the exagray value by 1.000000e+20. For example, 5 EGy × 1.000000e+20 = 5.000000e+20 rem.

Why do exagray and rem need converting at all?

Exa (E) represents 10^18, common in describing large-scale data and energy quantities. The rem, from the older CGS system, equals 0.01 sievert and is still used in US radiation regulation.

Is the exagray to rem conversion exact?

Yes. The relationship between exagray and rem is a fixed, defined ratio (× 1.000000e+20), so results are exact rather than a rounded estimate, limited only by the precision you choose to display.

Can I convert rem back to exagray?

Yes. Multiply the rem value by 1.000000e-20 (or divide by 1.000000e+20) to get exagray.

Does this conversion always hold exactly?

Only when the radiation weighting factor is 1, as it is for X-rays, gamma rays, and beta radiation. For alpha particles or neutrons, the equivalent dose in sieverts is higher than the absorbed dose in grays by that factor.

About this conversion

This page converts exagray (EGy) into rem (rem) within the radiation converter, using the fixed relationship 1 EGy = 1.000000e+20 rem.

Worked example: to convert 10 EGy to rem, multiply by the conversion factor: 10 × 1.000000e+20 = 1.000000e+21 rem.

A dose of 10 EGy corresponds to about 1.000000e+21 rem.

Reverse conversion: to go from rem back to exagray, divide by 1.000000e+20 (equivalently, multiply by 1.000000e-20).

Accuracy: this conversion uses a fixed, defined factor rather than a measured approximation, so results are exact to the precision shown; rounding only affects how many digits are displayed, not the underlying value.

Exagray: Exagray is 1000000000000000000 gray units. Rem: Rem is 0.01 sievert.

Background: Exa (E) represents 10^18, common in describing large-scale data and energy quantities.

Note: this conversion treats absorbed dose and equivalent dose as numerically equal, which assumes a radiation weighting factor of 1 (true for X-rays, gamma rays, and beta particles, but not for alpha particles or neutrons, which carry higher weighting factors).
