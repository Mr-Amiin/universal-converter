Frequently asked questions

What is the source unit in this conversion?

The source unit is the decigray (dGy). Decigray is 0.1 gray units.

What is the destination unit in this conversion?

The destination unit is the microsievert (uSv). Microsievert is 0.000001 sievert units.

How do I convert decigray to microsievert?

Multiply the decigray value by 100000. For example, 5 dGy × 100000 = 500000 uSv.

Why do decigray and microsievert need converting at all?

Deci (d) represents 10^-1, familiar from the decibel and deciliter. Micro (µ) represents 10^-6, common in biology, chemistry and electronics.

Is the decigray to microsievert conversion exact?

Yes. The relationship between decigray and microsievert is a fixed, defined ratio (× 100000), so results are exact rather than a rounded estimate, limited only by the precision you choose to display.

Can I convert microsievert back to decigray?

Yes. Multiply the microsievert value by 1.000000e-05 (or divide by 100000) to get decigray.

Does this conversion always hold exactly?

Only when the radiation weighting factor is 1, as it is for X-rays, gamma rays, and beta radiation. For alpha particles or neutrons, the equivalent dose in sieverts is higher than the absorbed dose in grays by that factor.

About this conversion

This page converts decigray (dGy) into microsievert (uSv) within the radiation converter, using the fixed relationship 1 dGy = 100000 uSv.

Worked example: to convert 10 dGy to uSv, multiply by the conversion factor: 10 × 100000 = 1000000 uSv.

A dose of 10 dGy corresponds to about 1000000 uSv.

Reverse conversion: to go from microsievert back to decigray, divide by 100000 (equivalently, multiply by 1.000000e-05).

Accuracy: this conversion uses a fixed, defined factor rather than a measured approximation, so results are exact to the precision shown; rounding only affects how many digits are displayed, not the underlying value.

Decigray: Decigray is 0.1 gray units. Microsievert: Microsievert is 0.000001 sievert units.

Background: Deci (d) represents 10^-1, familiar from the decibel and deciliter.

Note: this conversion treats absorbed dose and equivalent dose as numerically equal, which assumes a radiation weighting factor of 1 (true for X-rays, gamma rays, and beta particles, but not for alpha particles or neutrons, which carry higher weighting factors).
