Frequently asked questions

What is the source unit in this conversion?

The source unit is the kilogray (kGy). Kilogray is 1000 gray units.

What is the destination unit in this conversion?

The destination unit is the millisievert (mSv). Millisievert is 0.001 sievert units.

How do I convert kilogray to millisievert?

Multiply the kilogray value by 1000000. For example, 5 kGy × 1000000 = 5000000 mSv.

Why do kilogray and millisievert need converting at all?

Kilo (k) represents 10^3 and is the most familiar SI prefix in everyday use. Milli (m) represents 10^-3, one of the most common small-scale SI prefixes.

Is the kilogray to millisievert conversion exact?

Yes. The relationship between kilogray and millisievert is a fixed, defined ratio (× 1000000), so results are exact rather than a rounded estimate, limited only by the precision you choose to display.

Can I convert millisievert back to kilogray?

Yes. Multiply the millisievert value by 1.000000e-06 (or divide by 1000000) to get kilogray.

Does this conversion always hold exactly?

Only when the radiation weighting factor is 1, as it is for X-rays, gamma rays, and beta radiation. For alpha particles or neutrons, the equivalent dose in sieverts is higher than the absorbed dose in grays by that factor.

About this conversion

This page converts kilogray (kGy) into millisievert (mSv) within the radiation converter, using the fixed relationship 1 kGy = 1000000 mSv.

Worked example: to convert 10 kGy to mSv, multiply by the conversion factor: 10 × 1000000 = 10000000 mSv.

A dose of 10 kGy corresponds to about 10000000 mSv.

Reverse conversion: to go from millisievert back to kilogray, divide by 1000000 (equivalently, multiply by 1.000000e-06).

Accuracy: this conversion uses a fixed, defined factor rather than a measured approximation, so results are exact to the precision shown; rounding only affects how many digits are displayed, not the underlying value.

Kilogray: Kilogray is 1000 gray units. Millisievert: Millisievert is 0.001 sievert units.

Background: Kilo (k) represents 10^3 and is the most familiar SI prefix in everyday use.

Note: this conversion treats absorbed dose and equivalent dose as numerically equal, which assumes a radiation weighting factor of 1 (true for X-rays, gamma rays, and beta particles, but not for alpha particles or neutrons, which carry higher weighting factors).
