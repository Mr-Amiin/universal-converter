Frequently asked questions

What is the source unit in this conversion?

The source unit is the nanosievert (nSv). Nanosievert is 1e-9 sievert units.

What is the destination unit in this conversion?

The destination unit is the attogray (aGy). Attogray is 1e-18 gray units.

How do I convert nanosievert to attogray?

Multiply the nanosievert value by 1000000000. For example, 5 nSv × 1000000000 = 5000000000 aGy.

Why do nanosievert and attogray need converting at all?

Nano (n) represents 10^-9, familiar from nanotechnology and nanometers. Atto (a) represents 10^-18, used for atomic and subatomic-scale quantities.

Is the nanosievert to attogray conversion exact?

Yes. The relationship between nanosievert and attogray is a fixed, defined ratio (× 1000000000), so results are exact rather than a rounded estimate, limited only by the precision you choose to display.

Can I convert attogray back to nanosievert?

Yes. Multiply the attogray value by 1.000000e-09 (or divide by 1000000000) to get nanosievert.

Does this conversion always hold exactly?

Only when the radiation weighting factor is 1, as it is for X-rays, gamma rays, and beta radiation. For alpha particles or neutrons, the equivalent dose in sieverts is higher than the absorbed dose in grays by that factor.

About this conversion

This page converts nanosievert (nSv) into attogray (aGy) within the radiation converter, using the fixed relationship 1 nSv = 1000000000 aGy.

Worked example: to convert 10 nSv to aGy, multiply by the conversion factor: 10 × 1000000000 = 10000000000 aGy.

A dose of 10 nSv corresponds to about 10000000000 aGy.

Reverse conversion: to go from attogray back to nanosievert, divide by 1000000000 (equivalently, multiply by 1.000000e-09).

Accuracy: this conversion uses a fixed, defined factor rather than a measured approximation, so results are exact to the precision shown; rounding only affects how many digits are displayed, not the underlying value.

Nanosievert: Nanosievert is 1e-9 sievert units. Attogray: Attogray is 1e-18 gray units.

Background: Nano (n) represents 10^-9, familiar from nanotechnology and nanometers.

Note: this conversion treats absorbed dose and equivalent dose as numerically equal, which assumes a radiation weighting factor of 1 (true for X-rays, gamma rays, and beta particles, but not for alpha particles or neutrons, which carry higher weighting factors).
