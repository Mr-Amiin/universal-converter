Frequently asked questions

What is the source unit in this conversion?

The source unit is the centigray (cGy). Centigray is 0.01 gray units.

What is the destination unit in this conversion?

The destination unit is the sievert (Sv). Sievert is equivalent dose.

How do I convert centigray to sievert?

Multiply the centigray value by 0.01. For example, 5 cGy × 0.01 = 0.05 Sv.

Why do centigray and sievert need converting at all?

Centi (c) represents 10^-2, familiar from the centimeter. The sievert is the SI unit of equivalent radiation dose, weighting absorbed dose by biological effect.

Is the centigray to sievert conversion exact?

Yes. The relationship between centigray and sievert is a fixed, defined ratio (× 0.01), so results are exact rather than a rounded estimate, limited only by the precision you choose to display.

Can I convert sievert back to centigray?

Yes. Multiply the sievert value by 100 (or divide by 0.01) to get centigray.

Does this conversion always hold exactly?

Only when the radiation weighting factor is 1, as it is for X-rays, gamma rays, and beta radiation. For alpha particles or neutrons, the equivalent dose in sieverts is higher than the absorbed dose in grays by that factor.

About this conversion

This page converts centigray (cGy) into sievert (Sv) within the radiation converter, using the fixed relationship 1 cGy = 0.01 Sv.

Worked example: to convert 10 cGy to Sv, multiply by the conversion factor: 10 × 0.01 = 0.1 Sv.

A dose of 10 cGy corresponds to about 0.1 Sv.

Reverse conversion: to go from sievert back to centigray, divide by 0.01 (equivalently, multiply by 100).

Accuracy: this conversion uses a fixed, defined factor rather than a measured approximation, so results are exact to the precision shown; rounding only affects how many digits are displayed, not the underlying value.

Centigray: Centigray is 0.01 gray units. Sievert: Sievert is equivalent dose.

Background: Centi (c) represents 10^-2, familiar from the centimeter.

Note: this conversion treats absorbed dose and equivalent dose as numerically equal, which assumes a radiation weighting factor of 1 (true for X-rays, gamma rays, and beta particles, but not for alpha particles or neutrons, which carry higher weighting factors).
