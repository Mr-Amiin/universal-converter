Frequently asked questions

What is the source unit in this conversion?

The source unit is the microsievert (uSv). Microsievert is 0.000001 sievert units.

What is the destination unit in this conversion?

The destination unit is the teragray (TGy). Teragray is 1000000000000 gray units.

How do I convert microsievert to teragray?

Multiply the microsievert value by 1.000000e-18. For example, 5 uSv × 1.000000e-18 = 5.000000e-18 TGy.

Why do microsievert and teragray need converting at all?

Micro (µ) represents 10^-6, common in biology, chemistry and electronics. Tera (T) represents 10^12, familiar from terabyte-scale data storage.

Is the microsievert to teragray conversion exact?

Yes. The relationship between microsievert and teragray is a fixed, defined ratio (× 1.000000e-18), so results are exact rather than a rounded estimate, limited only by the precision you choose to display.

Can I convert teragray back to microsievert?

Yes. Multiply the teragray value by 1.000000e+18 (or divide by 1.000000e-18) to get microsievert.

Does this conversion always hold exactly?

Only when the radiation weighting factor is 1, as it is for X-rays, gamma rays, and beta radiation. For alpha particles or neutrons, the equivalent dose in sieverts is higher than the absorbed dose in grays by that factor.

About this conversion

A dose of 10 uSv corresponds to about 1.000000e-17 TGy.

Reverse conversion: to go from teragray back to microsievert, divide by 1.000000e-18 (equivalently, multiply by 1.000000e+18).

Accuracy: this conversion uses a fixed, defined factor rather than a measured approximation, so results are exact to the precision shown; rounding only affects how many digits are displayed, not the underlying value.

Microsievert: Microsievert is 0.000001 sievert units. Teragray: Teragray is 1000000000000 gray units.

Background: Micro (µ) represents 10^-6, common in biology, chemistry and electronics.

Note: this conversion treats absorbed dose and equivalent dose as numerically equal, which assumes a radiation weighting factor of 1 (true for X-rays, gamma rays, and beta particles, but not for alpha particles or neutrons, which carry higher weighting factors).
