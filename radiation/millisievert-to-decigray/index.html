Frequently asked questions

What is the source unit in this conversion?

The source unit is the millisievert (mSv). Millisievert is 0.001 sievert units.

What is the destination unit in this conversion?

The destination unit is the decigray (dGy). Decigray is 0.1 gray units.

How do I convert millisievert to decigray?

Multiply the millisievert value by 0.01. For example, 5 mSv × 0.01 = 0.05 dGy.

Why do millisievert and decigray need converting at all?

Milli (m) represents 10^-3, one of the most common small-scale SI prefixes. Deci (d) represents 10^-1, familiar from the decibel and deciliter.

Is the millisievert to decigray conversion exact?

Yes. The relationship between millisievert and decigray is a fixed, defined ratio (× 0.01), so results are exact rather than a rounded estimate, limited only by the precision you choose to display.

Can I convert decigray back to millisievert?

Yes. Multiply the decigray value by 100 (or divide by 0.01) to get millisievert.

Does this conversion always hold exactly?

Only when the radiation weighting factor is 1, as it is for X-rays, gamma rays, and beta radiation. For alpha particles or neutrons, the equivalent dose in sieverts is higher than the absorbed dose in grays by that factor.

About this conversion

This page converts millisievert (mSv) into decigray (dGy) within the radiation converter, using the fixed relationship 1 mSv = 0.01 dGy.

Worked example: to convert 10 mSv to dGy, multiply by the conversion factor: 10 × 0.01 = 0.1 dGy.

A dose of 10 mSv corresponds to about 0.1 dGy.

Reverse conversion: to go from decigray back to millisievert, divide by 0.01 (equivalently, multiply by 100).

Accuracy: this conversion uses a fixed, defined factor rather than a measured approximation, so results are exact to the precision shown; rounding only affects how many digits are displayed, not the underlying value.

Millisievert: Millisievert is 0.001 sievert units. Decigray: Decigray is 0.1 gray units.

Background: Milli (m) represents 10^-3, one of the most common small-scale SI prefixes.

Note: this conversion treats absorbed dose and equivalent dose as numerically equal, which assumes a radiation weighting factor of 1 (true for X-rays, gamma rays, and beta particles, but not for alpha particles or neutrons, which carry higher weighting factors).
