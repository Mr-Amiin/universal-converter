From definition

The coulomb (C) is the SI unit of electric charge, equal to the charge carried by a steady current of one ampere flowing for one second.

To definition

A Zettacoulomb (ZC) equals 1e+21 coulomb(s), a scaled unit of electric charge used for zetta-scale electrical work.

Formula

ZC = C × 1.000000e-21

How the formula works

Both coulomb and zettacoulomb measure the same physical quantity, so converting between them is a single multiplication. Multiply a value in coulombs by 1.000000e-21 to get zettacoulombs. To reverse the conversion, multiply a zettacoulomb value by 1.000000e+21 to get back to coulombs.

Frequently asked questions

How do I convert coulombs to zettacoulombs?

Multiply the coulomb value by 1 × 10-21. For example, 4 C × 1 × 10-21 = 4 × 10-21 ZC.

How do I convert zettacoulombs back to coulombs?

Divide the zettacoulomb value by 1 × 10-21, which is the same as multiplying by 1 × 1021.

Is the coulomb to zettacoulomb conversion exact?

Yes. Both are SI-prefixed units of charge, so the conversion factor is an exact power of ten rather than a rounded, measurement-based approximation.

Why would anyone need a unit as large as the zettacoulomb?

The zettacoulomb (ZC) mostly shows up in theoretical charge scales with no real engineered counterpart, where using the base coulomb would mean writing out unwieldy strings of digits.

What is a coulomb used for?

The coulomb (C) is commonly encountered in the charge moved by a phone battery over about an hour (a few thousand coulombs).

About this conversion

This page converts coulombs (C), the SI base unit of charge, equal to the charge transported by a one-ampere current in one second, into zettacoulombs (ZC), an SI-prefixed unit of charge equal to 1021 coulombs (zettacoulomb = zetta + coulomb).

This conversion is used when working with the charge moved by a phone battery over about an hour (a few thousand coulombs).

The formula is ZC = C × 1 × 10-21, a fixed decimal ratio between these two SI-prefixed units.

The coulomb is named after Charles-Augustin de Coulomb and was formally adopted at the 1881 International Electrical Congress in Paris.
